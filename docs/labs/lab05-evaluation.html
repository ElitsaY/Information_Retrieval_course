Overview of the lab

Lab 04 answered "How do we rank documents?" Lab 05 answers "How do we know whether that ranking is good?" The aim is not only to calculate the standard metrics, but to understand what user behaviour each one approximates. The systems compared are TF-IDF and BM25 from Lab 04, on Cranfield.

queries + system rankings + relevance judgments → metrics
  1. Why evaluation? Relevance judgments and the offline setup.
  2. Accuracy, Precision and Recall: why accuracy is not enough, and precision and recall at a cutoff \(k\).
  3. Why rank position matters: RR and MRR, AP and MAP.
  4. Graded relevance: DCG and nDCG.
  5. Different metrics can disagree: metric choice and per-query analysis.

Learning objectives

By the end you should be able to explain:

  • why retrieval evaluation requires relevance judgments;
  • why accuracy is not a useful measure for retrieval;
  • the difference between Precision and Recall, and why rank-aware metrics are needed;
  • the meaning of Reciprocal Rank and MRR, and of Average Precision and MAP;
  • how graded relevance leads to DCG and nDCG;
  • why different metrics can produce different conclusions;

Why evaluation?

Relevance judgments

For each query–document pair, a human assessor assigns a label: relevant or not relevant, or a grade such as 0 = not relevant, 1 = somewhat relevant, 2 = relevant, 3 = highly relevant. Once we have ranking + relevance, we can calculate metrics.

Part II

Accuracy, Precision and Recall

For one query, every document in the collection falls into one of four cells: the system retrieved it or not, and it is relevant or not.

relevantnot relevant
retrievedTP, true positive: a hitFP, false positive: noise the user reads
not retrievedFN, false negative: a missTN, true negative: correctly left out

Accuracy, and why it is not enough

Definition

Accuracy

\[ \text{Accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + FP + FN + TN} = \frac{\text{correct decisions}}{\text{all documents}} \]

predicted == actual

  • ❗ In retrieval, almost every document is not relevant. A Cranfield query has on average 7.2 relevant documents among 1,400 (1,612 relevant judgments for 225 queries). For a query with 7 relevant documents, a system that returns nothing leaves the other 1,393 correctly out and gets accuracy \(1393 / 1400 = 99.5\%\).
  • On the Web, with billions of pages and a handful of relevant ones, the useless empty system scores 99.9999…%. Accuracy is dominated by the true negatives, which the user never sees and does not care about.
  • So retrieval evaluation ignores TN and looks only at what the user sees (TP, FP) and what the user needs (TP, FN): precision and recall.

Try it: accuracy in three extreme cases

One query with 7 relevant documents. Pick a case to see its table and its accuracy.

A toy ranking

A toy ranking used throughout: D1 (relevant), D2 (not), D3 (relevant), D4 (not), D5 (relevant), written R N R N R. The collection contains exactly these three relevant documents: D1, D3, D5. For ranked retrieval both measures are computed at a cutoff \(k\): we look only at the top \(k\) results (P@1, P@5, P@10, …).

Precision

Definition

Precision

\[ \text{Precision} = \frac{\text{relevant retrieved}}{\text{retrieved}} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP} \qquad P@k = \frac{\text{relevant in the top } k}{k} \]

Of the documents we retrieved, how many are relevant? Precision looks only at what the system returned: how clean is the result list?

  • Example: a search engine shows 10 results and 4 of them are relevant: \(P@10 = 4/10 = 0.4\). Six of the ten results the user reads are wasted.
  • Documents below the cutoff do not matter for P@k, and neither do relevant documents the system never returned.

The toy ranking R N R N R, precision at every cutoff:

rank \(k\)12345
documentD1 RD2 ND3 RD4 ND5 R
relevant in top \(k\)11223
\(P@k\)1/1 = 1.001/2 = 0.502/3 = 0.672/4 = 0.503/5 = 0.60

Recall

Definition

Recall

\[ \text{Recall} = \frac{\text{relevant retrieved}}{\text{all relevant}} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \qquad Recall@k = \frac{\text{relevant in the top } k}{\text{all relevant}} \]

Of all relevant documents that exist, how many did we retrieve?

  • Example: a lawyer needs every court decision on a topic. The archive holds 50 relevant decisions and the search finds 40 of them: \(Recall = 40/50 = 0.8\), however many irrelevant ones came along.
  • ❗ If \(k\) is smaller than the number of relevant documents \(R\), even a perfect ranking cannot reach recall 1: the top \(k\) holds at most \(k\) relevant documents, so \(Recall@k \le k / R\). With \(R = 3\), a perfect ranking gets only \(Recall@2 = 2/3\). This is why recall is reported at a large \(k\) (for example Recall@100), or divided by \(\min(k, R)\) instead of \(R\) (capped recall).

The toy ranking R N R N R, with 3 relevant documents in the collection:

rank \(k\)12345
documentD1 RD2 ND3 RD4 ND5 R
relevant in top \(k\)11223
\(Recall@k\)1/3 = 0.331/3 = 0.332/3 = 0.672/3 = 0.673/3 = 1.00

Precision vs recall

  • They answer different questions. With 10 relevant documents: system A retrieves 2, both relevant: Precision 1.0, Recall 0.2. System B retrieves 100, 10 of them relevant: Precision 0.1, Recall 1.0. Neither measure is universally more important.
  • Retrieval depth: as we retrieve more and more documents, recall tends to increase and precision often decreases. This is a tendency, not a guarantee for every single step: a relevant document at rank \(k\) raises P@k.
?Question: a collection has 1,400 documents, 4 of them relevant to the query. What is the recall if the system returns the whole collection?

\(Recall = 4/4 = 1\): every relevant document is among the results. But \(Precision = 4/1400 \approx 0.003\): the 4 relevant documents are buried among 1,396 irrelevant ones. Perfect recall is easy if we ignore precision.

Part III

Why rank position matters

So far we judged one ranking for one query. A real search system answers thousands of queries, and the real question is usually: which system is better, TF-IDF or BM25, the old version or the new one? To answer it we score every query separately and then average, so each system gets one number that we can compare.

Many queries

a fixed test set, the same for every system

→
One score per query

from that query's ranking

→
Mean over queries

one number per system

→
Compare systems

higher mean = better on this test set

But the per-query score must also look at where the relevant documents are. Ranking A is R R R N N N N N N N, ranking B is N N N N N N N R R R. Both have \(P@10 = 0.3\), yet ranking A is clearly preferable for most users. We need rank-aware metrics, averaged over queries: MRR and MAP.

Reciprocal Rank and MRR

Definition

Reciprocal Rank

\[ RR = \frac{1}{r} \]

\(r\) is the rank of the first relevant result: at rank 1, \(RR = 1/1 = 1\); at rank 2, \(RR = 1/2\); at rank 5, \(RR = 1/5\). With no relevant result, RR = 0. RR scores one query.

Read \(RR = 1/2\) as: the first relevant result is at rank 2, so the user had to skip one useless result to reach it. The bigger the denominator, the further down the user had to look: \(1/1\) is the best, \(1/10\) means nine useless results came first.

Definition

Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR)

\[ MRR = \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{q \in Q} RR(q) = \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{q \in Q} \frac{1}{r_q} \]

MRR scores a whole system: how high, on average, it puts the first relevant result. It is between 0 and 1, and higher is better.

Example: three queries, each with its own ranking.

queryrankingfirst relevant at\(RR\)
Q1 youtubeR N Nrank 1\(1/1 = 1\)
Q2 capital of BulgariaN R Nrank 2\(1/2 = 0.5\)
Q3 who wrote HamletN N N Rrank 4\(1/4 = 0.25\)
\[ MRR = \frac{1/1 + 1/2 + 1/4}{3} = \frac{1.75}{3} \approx 0.583 \]
  • Why the mean? One query says little about a system. MRR sums up a whole set of test queries in one number, so two systems can be compared. An MRR near 1 means the first good answer is almost always at the top; an MRR of 0.1 means users typically scroll to around rank 10.
  • Useful when the user mainly wants one good result: navigational search, entity lookup, question answering, some FAQ tasks.
  • But MRR ignores everything after the first relevant document.

Average Precision and MAP

Definition

Average Precision

\[ AP = \frac{1}{R} \sum_{k=1}^{N} P@k \cdot rel(k) \qquad MAP = \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{q \in Q} AP(q) \]
  • \(R\) is the number of relevant documents for the query.
  • \(rel(k)\) is 1 if the document at rank \(k\) is relevant, 0 otherwise.
  • In words: every time we meet a relevant document, record the precision at that point; then average those values over all \(R\) relevant documents.
  • AP is for one query, MAP is the mean across queries.

Worked example: the toy ranking R N R N R, with \(R = 3\) relevant documents.

rank \(k\)12345
documentD1 RD2 ND3 RD4 ND5 R
\(rel(k)\)10101
\(P@k\)1/1 = 11/22/32/43/5
added to AP1–2/3–3/5
\[ AP = \frac{1 + 2/3 + 3/5}{3} \approx 0.756 \]

For rankings A and B above, AP is 1.0 and about 0.216, and RR is 1 and 0.125: the rank-aware metrics separate what P@10 could not.

When do we need MAP?

  • When the user needs many relevant documents, not just one. A student writing a literature review, a patent examiner checking whether an invention already exists, a doctor collecting all studies on a treatment: they read down the list and want every relevant document as high as possible.
  • MRR cannot see the difference here; MAP can. With 3 relevant documents, ranking X = R R R N N and ranking Y = R N N R R both have \(RR = 1\). But \(AP(X) = 1\) and \(AP(Y) = \frac{1 + 2/4 + 3/5}{3} = 0.7\): X puts all three relevant documents at the top.

Recap

MetricMain question
Precision@kHow many of the top \(k\) are relevant?
Recall@kHow much of the relevant material did we find?
RRWhere is the first relevant result?
MRRHow early is the first relevant result, on average?
APAre relevant documents consistently ranked high?
MAPMean AP across queries

So far relevance has been binary. What if some relevant documents are much better than others?

Part IV

Graded relevance: DCG and nDCG

When someone searches for something, not all search results are equally useful.

For example, imagine you search for "how to bake bread". You get four results:

  • Grade 3 (highly relevant): a complete bread recipe with ingredients and instructions.
  • Grade 2 (relevant): a page explaining how bread is made, but without a full recipe.
  • Grade 1 (somewhat relevant): a page that briefly mentions baking bread.
  • Grade 0 (not relevant): a page about buying shoes.

The problem with binary metrics

Traditional metrics like Precision@1 (P@1) only check whether the first result is relevant. They treat grades 1, 2 and 3 as equally relevant. So these two rankings get the same P@1:

positionRanking ARanking B
1Full recipe (grade 3)Brief mention (grade 1)
2Brief mention (grade 1)Full recipe (grade 3)

Both have \(P@1 = 1\), because their first result counts as relevant. But Ranking A is better, because the most useful result comes first.

How DCG solves this

DCG (Discounted Cumulative Gain) looks at two things:

  1. How useful a result is: higher relevance grades get higher scores.
  2. Where the result appears: results near the top count more, because users are more likely to look at them.

So DCG gives Ranking A a higher score than Ranking B.

The key idea

DCG rewards search engines for placing the most useful results at the top. Unlike binary metrics, it tells slightly useful results apart from very useful ones. The two metrics ask different questions:

  • Precision: "Did the search engine give me a relevant result?"
  • DCG: "Did the search engine give me the most useful results first?"

Three ideas in one formula

  • Gain: how useful is the document?
  • Discount: how far down in the ranking does it appear?
  • Cumulative: add the points from all documents.

DCG = give documents points for relevance, reduce their points if they appear lower, and add everything together.

Definition

Discounted Cumulative Gain

\[ DCG@k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{2^{rel_i} - 1}{\log_2(i + 1)} \qquad nDCG@k = \frac{DCG@k}{IDCG@k} \]

\(rel_i\) is the grade of the document at rank \(i\); \(IDCG@k\) is the DCG@k of the ideal ranking, all judged documents sorted by grade, highest first.

A worked example

Don't let the formula confuse you. It does just three things: gives points, reduces points, and adds them up.

1. Understanding each part

symbolmeaning
\(k\)Number of top search results we evaluate
\(i\)Position of a document (1st, 2nd, 3rd, …)
\(rel_i\)Relevance grade of the document at position \(i\)
\(2^{rel_i} - 1\)Gain: how many points the document earns
\(\log_2(i + 1)\)Discount: reduces points for lower-ranked documents
\(\sum\)Cumulative: add all the scores together

2. Simple example

Imagine a user searches for "how to bake bread" and receives three documents. We want to calculate DCG@3.

position \(i\)relevance \(rel_i\)gain \(2^{rel_i} - 1\)
13 (highly relevant)7
22 (relevant)3
31 (somewhat relevant)1

Step 1: calculate each document's discounted gain.

\[ \frac{7}{\log_2(2)} = 7 \qquad \frac{3}{\log_2(3)} \approx 1.89 \qquad \frac{1}{\log_2(4)} = 0.5 \]

Step 2: add everything together.

\[ DCG@3 = 7 + 1.89 + 0.5 = \boxed{9.39} \]
  • Why \(2^{rel_i} - 1\)? With this gain the rewards grow much faster than the grades: grade 1 earns 1 point, grade 2 earns 3, grade 3 earns 7.
  • Why \(\log_2(i + 1)\)? A logarithm grows slowly. So the penalty increases gradually, and lower-ranked documents keep part of their value.

IDCG (Ideal Discounted Cumulative Gain)

IDCG is the highest possible DCG score: the score you would get if all documents were ranked in the best possible order. In simple terms:

  • DCG: how good is your current ranking?
  • IDCG: how good would the perfect ranking be?

1. How does IDCG work?

Imagine a search engine returns 4 documents with these relevance grades:

positionDCG (current ranking)IDCG (ideal ranking)
1grade 1grade 3
2grade 3grade 2
3grade 0grade 1
4grade 2grade 0

IDCG puts the most relevant documents first:

Actual11233042
Ideal13223140

Both rankings contain the same documents. IDCG simply sorts them from highest to lowest relevance.

2. IDCG formula

\[ IDCG@k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{2^{rel_i^*} - 1}{\log_2(i + 1)} \]

This is the same formula as DCG. The only difference is the grade it uses:

  • \(rel_i\): relevance grade at position \(i\) in the actual ranking.
  • \(rel_i^*\): relevance grade at position \(i\) in the ideal ranking.

3. Calculating IDCG@3

Using the ideal ranking with grades [3, 2, 1]:

\[ IDCG@3 = \frac{7}{1} + \frac{3}{1.585} + \frac{1}{2} \approx 9.39 \]

This is the maximum DCG@3 for these documents.

4. Why do we need IDCG?

DCG alone does not tell us how close a ranking is to perfect. For example, is a DCG score of 7 good or bad? We cannot know without comparing it to the ideal score. That is why we use IDCG to calculate nDCG (normalized DCG):

\[ nDCG@k = \frac{DCG@k}{IDCG@k} \]

If your DCG is 7 and your IDCG is 10:

\[ nDCG = \frac{7}{10} = 0.7 \]

This means your ranking gets 70% of the ideal DCG score.

Key takeaway

  • DCG: score of your actual ranking.
  • IDCG: score of the perfect ranking.
  • nDCG: how close your actual ranking is to the perfect ranking.

Try it: compute nDCG

Type the grades of a ranking, top first. The tables show every term of the sum; "Sort to ideal" turns the ranking into the ideal one.

💡 Not every nDCG uses \(2^{rel} - 1\). A common variant (the trec_eval definition) uses the grade itself as the gain. For the playground's default ranking [3, 0, 2, 1], the formula above gives 0.951 and the grade-as-gain variant gives 0.930. Always report which one you used.

Part V

Different metrics can disagree

  • System A has a higher P@10, system B a higher Recall@100: no contradiction, they are strong in different ways.
  • System A has a higher MRR, system B a higher MAP: this can happen, because MRR cares about the first relevant result and MAP about all of them.

Metric choice follows the task

TaskWhat mattersTypical metrics
Web searchthe early ranksP@5, P@10, nDCG@10
Legal or medical literature searchfinding as much relevant material as possibleRecall@100, Recall@1000
Navigational searchone correct result, quicklyMRR
General ad-hoc retrievalthe whole rankingMAP, nDCG

Per-query analysis

Averages can hide failures. A higher MAP for one system does not mean it wins on every query: some queries may strongly prefer one system, others the other. Serious IR evaluation combines aggregate metrics + per-query analysis + error analysis.
