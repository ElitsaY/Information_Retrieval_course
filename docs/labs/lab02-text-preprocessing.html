Main steps for building an index

See it with the notebook's example document

Tokenization

Tokenization

Split a document into sentences, then each sentence into tokens. Every decision here (keep km/h together? split can't?) changes which terms end up in the index.

Sentences: Punkt

PunktSentenceTokenizer splits the example document into its 4 sentences. Punkt is unsupervised: it learns abbreviations, collocations and typical sentence starters from a corpus of text.

  • PunktSentenceTokenizer(): the notebook's call, with no training text, so it knows no abbreviations.
  • sent_tokenize(text): loads the Punkt model pre-trained on English (punkt_tab). Lab 01's preprocess_document uses it.

Try it: split into sentences

The outputs were computed with NLTK 3.9. Compare the two models on Abbreviations; on Newsgroup post (a real sci.crypt message), training doesn't help.

Words: four NLTK tokenizers

  • WhitespaceTokenizer: splits only on spaces, tabs and newlines, so punctuation stays attached (Portugal,).
  • WordPunctTokenizer: the regex \w+|[^\w\s]+: runs of letters and digits, and runs of punctuation.
  • TreebankWordTokenizer: follows the Penn Treebank conventions. It splits contractions (ca n't), separates most punctuation, and keeps 50,000 and 366.88 whole. It expects one sentence at a time, so only a final period is split off.
  • TweetTokenizer: uses regexes for URLs, emoticons, @handles, #hashtags, e-mails and phone numbers, and then for words.

from nltk.tokenize import PunktSentenceTokenizer
from nltk.tokenize import TweetTokenizer, WhitespaceTokenizer, WordPunctTokenizer, TreebankWordTokenizer

example_document = """
Hurricane-force winds have struck central and northern Portugal, leaving 300,000 homes without power.

The remnants of Hurricane Leslie swept in overnight on Saturday, with winds gusting up to 176km/h (109mph).

Civil defence officials said 27 people suffered minor injuries, with localised flooding, hundreds of trees uprooted and a number of flights cancelled.

The storm, one of the most powerful to ever hit the country, is now passing over northern Spain.
"""
sent_tokenizer = PunktSentenceTokenizer()
sentences = sent_tokenizer.tokenize(example_document)   # 4 sentences

tweet_tok, whsp_tok, wordpunct_tok, treebank_tok = TweetTokenizer(), WhitespaceTokenizer(), WordPunctTokenizer(), TreebankWordTokenizer()

print(whsp_tok.tokenize(sentences[0]))
# ['Hurricane-force', 'winds', 'have', 'struck', 'central', 'and', 'northern', 'Portugal,', 'leaving', '300,000', 'homes', 'without', 'power.']
print(wordpunct_tok.tokenize('On a $50,000 mortgage of 30 years at 8 percent, the monthly payment would be $366.88.'))
# ['On', 'a', '$', '50', ',', '000', 'mortgage', ..., 'be', '$', '366', '.', '88', '.']
print(treebank_tok.tokenize('On a $50,000 mortgage of 30 years at 8 percent, the monthly payment would be $366.88.'))
# ['On', 'a', '$', '50,000', 'mortgage', ..., 'be', '$', '366.88', '.']
print(treebank_tok.tokenize('My email is admin@sth.com'))    # ['My', 'email', 'is', 'admin', '@', 'sth.com']
print(tweet_tok.tokenize('My email is admin@sth.com'))       # ['My', 'email', 'is', 'admin@sth.com']
print(tweet_tok.tokenize('Last sunny day before winter :) #sunisenergy'))
# ['Last', 'sunny', 'day', 'before', 'winter', ':)', '#sunisenergy']
print(tweet_tok.tokenize('Here is my ip address 192.33.24.45'))   # we might want to fix this
# ['Here', 'is', 'my', 'ip', 'address', '192.33', '.', '24.45']
print(tweet_tok.tokenize("I can't stop singing that song!"))     # we might want to split can't in can, 't instead
# ['I', "can't", 'stop', 'singing', 'that', 'song', '!']
1Exercise: play with the tokenizers and find more cases where they fail.

URLs, e-mails, file names: WordPunct and Treebank cut a URL into pieces that match unrelated words. Treebank also splits e-mails at @.

Hyphens and dates: state-of-the-art, York-based, 2024-09-27: one token or several? Tweet produces 2024-09- 27.

Names with symbols: C++, C#, Node.js, O'Neill, AT&T.

Abbreviations: U.S.A., e.g., p.m. are spelled out letter by letter, and they also confuse sentence splitting.

Numbers: IP addresses, phone numbers, times (10:30am), prices (€25.50), units (176km/h).

Apostrophes: can't, won't, workstation's, and the typographic ’ in c’è.

Other languages: German compounds and Chinese or Japanese text have no spaces between words and need a word segmenter.

There is no single right answer: choose what the queries of your users need, and apply the same tokenizer to documents and queries.

Normalization

Normalization

How we normalize the text depends on the domain and the task. Some normalization steps:

  • Irrelevant parts: whole chunks of the document might be irrelevant, e.g. HTML tags.
  • Numbers: remove them or replace them with a common token.
  • Other token types we don't care about, e.g. URLs, @ mentions, 😀: remove or replace them the same way.
  • Lower-case all words, or even use a tool to upper-case all named entities, like geographic names.
  • Equivalence classes for matching synonyms, abbreviations, etc.
  • Stopwords: remove them. The list is usually made by hand.
  • Google N-Grams: a huge collection of frequency counts of word n-grams in web pages, for many languages. Use it to see which words are used most often.
  • Stemming: crudely remove prefixes and suffixes, so that different forms of a word match, like car-sharing and car sharing.
  • Lemmatization: replace the word by its lemma (its dictionary form).

2Removing HTML tags and other chunks

Tags carry structure: words in <title> or <h1> matter more than words in the footer (zones, Lab 01). On a site about web development, <table> may be exactly what users search for. In the newsgroup posts, dropping the headers also drops the Subject, the best summary of the post.

3Removing or replacing numbers

Numbers are often the query: Windows 95, COVID-19, iPhone 15, 1984 (the novel), error 404, a year in news search, a product code. In sci.crypt, the query 1993 is left with no terms (see querying).

4Removing URLs, @ mentions and emoji

Users search for a domain (nltk.org) or a person (@nlp_lab). Emoji carry sentiment and meaning (😀 vs 😡) that sentiment analysis needs. Splitting a URL into words (en, wikipedia, org) is often better than removing it.

5Lower-casing

Case separates meanings: US / us, WHO / who, IT / it, Apple / apple, Bush / bush, DES (the cipher) / des. After lower-casing, WHO and IT are even removed as stopwords.

6Equivalence classes (synonyms, abbreviations)

Synonyms depend on context: jaguar = car only for some queries. C.A.T. (a company) is not cat, and Windows is not windows. Expanding every query with its synonyms raises recall but lowers precision.

7Removing stopwords

Phrases made of stopwords: To be or not to be, the bands The Who and Take That. Meaning carried by stopwords: flights to London vs flights from London, negation not good. Sentiment analysis needs not. In sci.crypt, not secure becomes just secure.

8Stemming

Over-stemming merges unrelated words: university, universe → univers; organization, organ → organ; general, generator → gener (Porter); data, date → dat and news → new (Lancaster). Under-stemming misses irregular forms: children / child, mice / mouse, ran / run. Names get stemmed too: Portugal → portug.

9Lemmatization

It needs the part of speech: lemmatize('meeting') is meeting (a noun), lemmatize('meeting', 'v') is meet. With the default noun reading, lemmatize('was') gives wa and lemmatize('us') gives u. better → good works only with pos='a'. It is slower than stemming, and words that are not in the dictionary (typos, names) are left unchanged.

Stemming

Stemming and lemmatization

Resources: Introduction to stemming (GeeksforGeeks) and A Comparative Study of Stemming Algorithms (Jivani, 2011).

📐 Stemmer vs lemmatizer

A stemmer cuts suffixes with rules and no dictionary. The result does not have to be a word (automobil, eventu). A lemmatizer looks the word up (NLTK uses WordNet) and returns a dictionary form (pollutions → pollution). It needs the part of speech and leaves unknown words, such as the notebook's typo decreas, as they are.

Where stemming is used

  • Information retrieval systems like search engines.
  • Domain analysis: determining domain vocabularies.
  • Indexing: mapping documents that use different forms of a word to common subjects.
  • Sentiment analysis of reviews and comments (e.g. for online retail stores): stemming is a text-preparation step.
  • Document clustering (text clustering): subject extraction, automatic document structuring, fast information retrieval.

Popular stemmers

StemmerIdea✅ Advantage❌ Limitation
PorterRemoves the common morphological and inflectional endings of English words, in five steps of rules.Best output compared to other stemmers, lower error rate.The stems are not always real words.
LovinsRemoves the longest suffix, then recodes the stem to turn it into a valid word.Fast; handles irregular plurals like teeth and tooth.Slow, and often fails to turn the stems into words.
DawsonSuffixes stored in reversed order, indexed by their length and last letter.Fast; covers more suffixes.Very complex to implement.
KrovetzPlural → singular, past tense → present, removes -ing.Light; can be a pre-stemmer for other stemmers.Inefficient for large documents.
SnowballAlso called Porter2: improves on Porter and has versions for many languages.Can stem non-English words too.Still rule-based: over- and under-stemming.
N-gramCompares words by their shared character n-grams.Based on string comparisons; language independent.Needs space to create and index the n-grams, and is slow.

💡 The notebook calls the N-gram stemmer "language dependent". The cited survey (Jivani, 2011, Table 2) lists it as language independent: it uses only string statistics, with no rules for a particular language. The Lancaster (Paice/Husk) stemmer used below is the most aggressive of NLTK's three.

nltk.download('wordnet')

from nltk.stem.porter import PorterStemmer
from nltk.stem import WordNetLemmatizer, porter, lancaster, snowball
from nltk.corpus import stopwords
from nltk.tokenize import TweetTokenizer
from collections import defaultdict
import pandas as pd

tweet_tok = TweetTokenizer()

stemmer = PorterStemmer()
lemmatizer = WordNetLemmatizer()
tokens = tweet_tok.tokenize('Car sharing will bring a whole new era to the automobile industry. Eventually, it may even decreas air pollutions.')
for token in tokens:
    print(token, lemmatizer.lemmatize(token), stemmer.stem(token))
# Car Car car / sharing sharing share / automobile automobile automobil / industry industry industri
# Eventually Eventually eventu / decreas decreas decrea / pollutions pollution pollut ...

print(len(stopwords.words('english')))   # 179 in the notebook's NLTK data, 198 in current NLTK data

stem_list = [porter.PorterStemmer(), lancaster.LancasterStemmer(), snowball.SnowballStemmer('english')]
examples = ['agreed', 'children', 'flies', 'humbled', 'colonizer', 'owned', 'meeting', 'sitting', 'understood', 'whom']
result = defaultdict(list)
for word in examples:
    result['original'].append(word)
    for s in stem_list:   # the notebook reuses the name `stemmer` here
        result[s.__class__.__name__].append(s.stem(word))
print(pd.DataFrame(result).to_markdown())

tokenize_documents.py Python ▾ 
from nltk.tokenize import sent_tokenize, TweetTokenizer
from string import punctuation
from os import scandir
from collections import Counter
from pandas import DataFrame
from nltk.corpus import stopwords
from nltk.stem.porter import PorterStemmer
tw_tokenizer = TweetTokenizer()

def tokenize_documents(path, tokenizer):
    """
    Returns a list of tokenized documents: lower-cased tokens without punctuation.
    """
    documents = []
    for entry in sorted(scandir(path), key=lambda e: e.name):
        with open(entry.path, errors='ignore') as f:
            content = f.read()
        tokens = []
        for sentence in sent_tokenize(content):
            tokens += [tok.lower() for tok in tokenizer.tokenize(sentence) if tok not in punctuation]
        documents.append(tokens)
    return documents

print(tokenize_documents('data/mini_newsgroups/sci.crypt/', tw_tokenizer)[1][:12])
# ['path', 'cantaloupe.srv.cs.cmu.edu', 'das-news.harvard.edu', 'ogicse', 'uwm.edu', 'zaphod.mps.ohio-state.edu',
#  'howland.reston.ans.net', 'sol.ctr.columbia.edu', 'news.cs.columbia.edu', 'ji', 'from', 'ji@cs.columbia.edu']

# how the most frequent tokens change
word_freqs = Counter(sum(tokenize_documents('data/mini_newsgroups/sci.crypt/', tw_tokenizer), []))
df = DataFrame(list(word_freqs.items()), columns=['text', 'freq'])
df.sort_values(['freq'], inplace=True, ascending=False)
print(df.head())                 # the 1718, to 831, of 711, a 563, and 506

en_stopwords = set(stopwords.words('english'))
df_no_stopwords = df[~df['text'].isin(en_stopwords)].copy()
print(df_no_stopwords.head())    # sci.crypt 158, cantaloupe.srv.cs.cmu.edu 150, encryption 148, key 133, clipper 115

df_no_stopwords['stemmed'] = df_no_stopwords['text'].apply(PorterStemmer().stem)
stemmed_freqs = df_no_stopwords.groupby(['stemmed'])['freq'].sum()
stemmed_freqs.sort_values(inplace=True, ascending=False)
print(stemmed_freqs.head())      # key 198, encrypt 187, use 185, sci.crypt 158, cantaloupe.srv.cs.cmu.edu 150

print(len(df), len(df_no_stopwords), len(stemmed_freqs))
# 5769 5624 4592 with the notebook's 179 stopwords; 5769 5612 4580 with the 198 of current NLTK data

Querying

Querying with the same preprocessing

Your query methods must apply the same steps to the query words. A query term matches only if it went through the same tokenizer, case folding, stopword list and stemmer as the documents.

  • Positive: stemming and lemmatization match other forms of a word (higher recall).
  • Negative: unrelated words merge, and removed words (stopwords, numbers) make parts of the query disappear (lower precision, or no answer).

Summary: key takeaways

  1. Pipeline: documents → tokenizer → linguistic modules → indexer. Documents and queries must go through the same steps.
  2. Sentences: Punkt needs a trained model (sent_tokenize) to know abbreviations such as Mr. and U.S.
  3. Tokenizers: Whitespace, WordPunct, Treebank and Tweet disagree on URLs, e-mails, numbers, contractions and emoji. Pick one for your data and queries.
  4. Normalization: remove irrelevant parts, numbers and token types; lower-case; equivalence classes; stopwords. Each step also has cases where it hurts.
  5. Stemming: rule-based suffix removal (Porter, Snowball, Lancaster). It is cheap and raises recall, but over-stems (university = universe) and under-stems (children ≠ child).
  6. Lemmatization: dictionary forms via WordNet; needs the part of speech.
  7. On sci.crypt: stopwords and Porter take the dictionary from 5,769 to 4,592 terms. Header noise (sci.crypt, host names) needs corpus-specific cleaning.
